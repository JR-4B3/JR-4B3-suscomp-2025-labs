# Sustainable Digital Interaction Design Analysis: LifeCache

## Part A Core SDID Analysis

### (E1) Does the design encourage infrastructural expansion or obsolescence?

Yes. LifeCache requires new AI processing infrastructure in data centers and substantial storage capacity for lifetime archives. As video quality improves, users will expect upgrades, driving continual infrastructure replacement. This exemplifies the cornucopian principles of HIGH QUALITY (C5) and ETERNAL (C8).

### (E2) Does the design encourage increased infrastructural use?

Yes. Continuous uploading from multiple devices, AI processing for each search query, and real time synchronization across devices all drive heavy infrastructure use. Peak demand during evenings and weekends stresses the network, pushing further expansion. This combines CONTINUOUS ACCESS (C7), PERVASIVE (C6), and INSTANT (C3).

### (E3) Does the design mitigate or reduce infrastructural use in some way?

No. LifeCache has no built in mitigation. It could be improved through off peak uploads, aggressive deduplication, local search processing, or default lower quality settings, but the current design offers none of these.

### (E4) Does the design encourage digital waste, or avoidance of it?

Yes, it encourages waste. Automatic capture leads to the accumulation of large volumes of ephemeral content such as blurry photos, background noise, and routine location data that is never accessed but stored indefinitely. This combines the EPHEMERAL (C9) and ETERNAL (C8) paradox, creating storage and energy waste without corresponding value.

### (E5) Does the design promote the sharing of infrastructure?

No. LifeCache is purely personal, with each user maintaining a separate archive. This prevents infrastructure sharing compared to family or community archives. Following the PERSONAL principle (C1), it maximizes rather than minimizes infrastructure demands.

## Summary

LifeCache embodies unsustainable cornucopian design. It expands infrastructure, increases use, generates digital waste, and discourages sharing, scoring poorly across all five SDID questions.

# Part B Computing Within Limits, Collapse Informatics, and Responsible Design

## (R1) Global environmental impact and planetary boundaries

The impact would be catastrophic at global scale. With over eight billion potential users, LifeCache would require exascale storage infrastructure and continuous AI processing. Even conservative assumptions produce multiple exabytes of new data annually, alongside substantial energy costs for storage, retrieval, and computation.

Within planetary boundaries, this is not viable. Data centers already consume a significant proportion of global electricity, and scaling LifeCache would dramatically increase this demand. The service fundamentally contradicts computing within limits by assuming infinite growth capacity that planetary boundaries cannot sustain.

Assumption: Even with aggressive renewable energy adoption, the raw materials required for server manufacturing, cooling water use, and e waste from infrastructure turnover would exceed sustainable extraction rates.

## (R2) Robustness with reduced infrastructure availability

LifeCache is extremely fragile. It is cloud dependent by design, and without continuous connectivity or access to data centers, core functionality fails. Users cannot search, retrieve, or summarize their stored memories.

Assumption: Under rolling blackouts, bandwidth rationing, or network instability, conditions increasingly likely under climate stress, the service would become unreliable or unusable. Unlike local first systems, it offers no meaningful graceful degradation.

## (R3) Business model growth assumptions and risks

The business model assumes perpetual growth. Revenue is likely tied to subscription tiers based on storage volume, requiring continual infrastructure expansion to support both new users and the ever growing archives of existing ones.

Key risks include stranded assets under carbon regulation, rising energy prices that undermine economic viability, and regulatory restrictions on high impact digital services.

Assumption: Investor expectations of continuous growth create pressure to expand infrastructure regardless of environmental cost, reinforcing the cornucopian growth feedback loop.

## (R4) Societal value and justification scenarios

Societal value is limited. LifeCache prioritizes convenience and nostalgia rather than essential human needs. Unlike healthcare or emergency systems, it does not justify large scale infrastructure use.

Restricted scenarios such as medical memory support or legal documentation may offer limited justification, but only with highly targeted data capture. Even in these cases, simpler and lower impact alternatives would likely suffice. In most scenarios, environmental cost outweighs societal benefit.

## (R5) Restricted version and collapse scenarios

A restricted MemoryLite version could store only user curated moments, enforce strict lifetime storage limits, process searches locally on device, support shared family archives, and rely on peer to peer synchronization rather than centralized data centers.

Such a version would dramatically reduce infrastructure impact while preserving limited memory support. It could function under intermittent connectivity and material constraints.

Assumption: In conditions of full societal collapse, even this restricted version becomes a luxury, but it would remain viable longer than the original design.

## (R6) Healthy relationship with digital technology

LifeCache promotes unhealthy dependency through cognitive offloading, continuous mediation of lived experience, and anxiety related to data loss or system failure.

Assumption: Marketing claims of reduced cognitive burden obscure the increased mental load of managing, maintaining, and relying on a lifelong digital archive.

A healthier alternative would encourage selective and intentional remembering while accepting forgetting as a natural and valuable human process.

## (R7) Personal values as a designer

This service conflicts with responsible design values. The environmental costs of infrastructure expansion, continuous energy consumption, and e waste generation are not justified by benefits such as convenience or nostalgia.

LifeCache represents technological solutionism applied to human memory, externalizing ecological harm while privatizing convenience. As a designer, I cannot justify a system whose planetary scale deployment would accelerate ecological breakdown for marginal personal benefit.

An alternative approach would focus on tools that support meaningful engagement with selected memories, communal sharing, and acceptance of natural forgetting, embodying principles of sufficiency, longevity, and care rather than expansion and accumulation.

## Reflections

The ethical viability of digital services is fundamentally shaped by constraints on energy, materials, and human attention. These are hard limits rather than temporary barriers to be overcome through innovation. LifeCache fails to acknowledge these limits by assuming unlimited energy for continuous processing, unlimited materials for infrastructure expansion, and unlimited human attention to manage an ever growing archive.

However, the acceptability of infrastructure intensity varies with scale and purpose. A medical system serving a small population may justify high per user costs, while a mass market convenience service cannot. Responsible designers must therefore ask not only whether a service can scale, but whether it should scale. In the case of LifeCache, global deployment would be ecologically irresponsible, requiring either radical restriction of scope or abandonment of the concept entirely.
